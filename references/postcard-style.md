# Postcard Style Reference

## Default ranges

- Photo area: 52–88% of the card, depending on full-bleed, inset, torn, or masked treatment.
- Quiet paper inside an illustration field: 35–75%.
- Outer postcard margin: 3–9%, or full bleed for 1950s offset cards.
- Torn fiber band when present: 1–3% of the short edge.
- Accent ink: 4–14%; do not force one accent when the period palette already provides hierarchy.
- Micro-text: 1.3–2.2% of card height.

## Source-aware abstraction

- People and animals: preserve count, anatomy, posture, gaze, spacing, clothing, and accessories. Abstract only the surrounding field.
- Landscapes: preserve horizon, ridge, shoreline, path, reflection, scale, and one source-specific contour.
- Foliage: merge 85–95% of leaves and twigs into one silhouette mass and two or three directional gestures.
- Snow, ice, and water: use a field-led halftone mass with a few broken contours or reflection rhythms.
- Architecture: preserve perspective and defining openings; continue one wall, roofline, or shadow beyond the tear.
- Require at least two source-derived environmental cues in the poster background. One must preserve a real contour or directional rhythm from the photo.

## Aging map

- Paper scale: visible fibers, uneven warm base, sparse foxing, edge oxidation, and subtle compression or dents.
- Print scale: localized fading, coarse halftone or lithographic grain, dry ink, plate wear, color drift, and small registration errors.
- Handling scale: rubbed corners, hairline scratches, thumb-softened areas, crease memory, and uneven exposure.
- Use at least one mechanism from each scale. Place wear asymmetrically and respond to image structure; never overlay uniform noise.
- Keep whites warm but varied. Avoid monochrome brown washes, heavy grunge overlays, fake burn marks, and illegible distress.

## Postal system

- Place `POST CARD` or `CARTE POSTALE` where it balances the image; upper-right is only one option.
- Use one source-specific perforated stamp or printed postal block. Derive its silhouette from the actual subject, relationship, object, ridge, shoreline, trail, canopy, reflection, or architecture in the photo.
- Reinterpret the motif using the selected era's print method; do not paste a small rectangular copy of the source photo.
- Coordinate stamp frame shape and placement with the dominant environmental contour and negative space.
- Use a partial circular cancellation, wavy bars, route line, serial ornament, or plate mark. Keep it subordinate.
- Omit real postal metadata unless the user supplies it.

## Palette logic

Build two to four inks from the selected vintage direction and the source. Intensify a beak, jacket, flower, sign, reflection, or other meaningful minor color when useful. Cool scenes may use faded vermilion, mustard, dusty rose, tobacco, or no warm accent at all. Let color follow a real path, gaze, waterline, ridge, wing direction, reflection, or repeated step.

## Quality gate

- The source is immediately recognizable.
- Subject count and relationships are unchanged.
- The photo remains natural and detailed.
- One primary period direction is clearly legible.
- At least three aging mechanisms are visible across paper, print, and handling scales.
- The two-to-four-ink palette feels printed rather than digitally filtered.
- Any torn edge is fibrous, narrow, irregular, and flat; torn paper is not mandatory.
- Postal marks and micro-text are present but subordinate and composition-aware.
- The stamp motif is traceable to a distinctive feature in the source and differs meaningfully across batch items.
- The poster background visibly continues at least two source cues while remaining simplified and spacious.
- Text is correctly spelled.
- No mockup, address back, invented metadata, logo, or watermark appears.
- Batch outputs read as one series while varying print method, crop device, palette emphasis, postal placement, and wear pattern.
