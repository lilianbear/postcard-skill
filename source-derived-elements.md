# Source-Derived Elements

Design the stamp and poster background from the uploaded photograph. Treat both as scene interpretation, never interchangeable decoration.

## Feature extraction

Identify these before prompting:

- Primary anchor: the subject or landform that must dominate the photographic area.
- Stamp seed: one feature recognizable at thumbnail size with a strong silhouette or gesture.
- Environmental contour: one line that identifies the place, such as a ridge, shore, ice shelf, canopy opening, trail, roofline, or horizon.
- Directional rhythm: gaze, walking direction, wing angle, wave, reflection, hanging icicles, tree growth, or repeated steps.
- Native accent: a small meaningful source color suitable for print emphasis.

Do not use the same feature as both the full photographic anchor and an identical miniature copy. Compress, crop, pair, rotate, or reframe the stamp seed so it works as an emblem.

## Stamp routing

- Single animal or person: use the profile, head-and-shoulders contour, foot or paw trace, characteristic accessory, or one defining gesture.
- Pair or group: use the negative space between figures, facing gesture, linked movement, or simplified count rhythm; preserve the correct number when legible.
- Mountain or glacier: use the exact ridge or ice-front profile, not a generic triangular mountain.
- Forest or hiking trail: use a trail switchback, stair rhythm, canopy window, distinctive leaf cluster, boot tread, or backpack silhouette.
- Water or coast: use the shoreline, reflection pair, wave interval, iceberg profile, or vessel contour.
- Architecture: use a defining opening, roofline, facade module, tower, or shadow profile.
- Object-led scene: use the object's defining geometry or its shadow, not an invented travel icon.

Render the motif with the selected period method: engraved linework for early cards, two-plate lithography for 1930s, coarse offset rosette for 1950s, or rough stencil screenprint for 1960s. Let perforation shape and frame proportions echo source geometry when possible.

## Background routing

- Snow and ice: extend layered blue-gray plates, hanging icicle rhythms, wind-scoured contours, crystalline halftone, or a reflected water band.
- Forest: extend a dark canopy mass, diagonal leaf or bamboo gestures, vertical trunks, damp trail contour, and filtered-light gaps.
- Mountains: continue the true ridge hierarchy, slope direction, valley opening, atmospheric bands, and trail movement.
- Water: continue the horizon, reflection axis, ripple rhythm, shoreline curve, and cold-to-warm color drift.
- Wildlife close-up: abstract habitat texture and gaze direction; keep anatomy photographic and let the background carry feathers, snow granules, stone, grass, or water rhythm.
- People and journeys: continue walking direction, spacing, path or steps, clothing accent, map-like route rhythm, and destination-facing negative space.
- Architecture: continue perspective lines, wall planes, openings, cast shadows, and street rhythm.

Use two or three source cues, not all of them. Build one dominant background mass and one supporting rhythm. Preserve negative space so the result reads as a designed vintage poster.

## Coherence rules

- Make the stamp, cancellation, background, and accent color participate in one eye path.
- Let the cancellation echo a real direction from the source, such as a shoreline, trail, gaze, wing, ridge, or reflection.
- Keep the stamp simpler and higher-contrast than the background.
- Let background abstraction cross or meet the photographic boundary at one meaningful place.
- Avoid literal scenic wallpaper, generic travel clip-art, modern vector gradients, stock icons, and unrelated ornamental maps.
- In batch work, preserve the series' era while deriving a different motif and background grammar from each source.
